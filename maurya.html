322 BCE  —  185 BCE

The Empire That United Much of India

From the fall of the Nanda dynasty to the edicts of Ashoka, the Maurya Empire bound together the Indian subcontinent under one administration for the first time — and left behind pillars, ideas, and a system of governance that outlasted the empire itself.

c. 6th – 4th century BCE

Before the Mauryas

India's political map was fractured long before Chandragupta — a patchwork of kingdoms and republics competing for territory along the Ganges plain.

  • Mahajanapadas

    Sixteen major kingdoms and republics spread across northern India, each with its own army, capital, and ambitions.

  • Magadha

    The most powerful of these states, centred on the fertile Gangetic plain — rich in resources and strategically placed.

  • Nanda Dynasty

    Ruled Magadha with a vast treasury and an intimidating army, but grew resented for heavy taxation.

  • Rise of Pataliputra

    The Nanda capital grew into the subcontinent's most fortified and prosperous city — the prize Chandragupta would later seize.

The Mahajanapadas, before unification
c. 322 BCE

The Rise of Chandragupta Maurya

A young claimant, a brilliant strategist, and a fallen dynasty — the founding of the Maurya Empire began with an alliance that reshaped a subcontinent.

Chandragupta Maurya

Rose from obscurity to overthrow the Nanda king and found a new imperial line, later expanding it west against the successors of Alexander.

Chanakya / Kautilya

The strategist and teacher behind the throne, whose statecraft — later recorded in the Arthashastra — shaped the empire's administration.

Defeat of the Nandas

A campaign of alliances and guerrilla tactics brought down the entrenched Nanda dynasty and delivered Pataliputra to Chandragupta.

Bindusara

Extends Mauryan control further into the Deccan, maintaining ties with Hellenistic kingdoms to the west.

Comes to power after a contested succession and continues the empire's expansion at its territorial peak.

The brutal Kalinga War leads Ashoka to renounce further conquest and embrace the Buddhist principle of dhamma.

Later Mauryas

Weaker successors struggle to hold the empire together as regional governors gain independence.

The last Mauryan ruler, Brihadratha, is overthrown, ending a century and a half of imperial rule.

Chandragupta's Empire

At its height, the empire stretched from the Hindu Kush in the northwest to the Bay of Bengal in the east — the largest political unit the subcontinent had yet seen.

  • PataliputraImperial capital on the Ganges, seat of the royal court.
  • Major territoriesFrom modern Afghanistan to Bengal, and south into the Deccan.
  • Administrative centresProvincial capitals governed by royal princes and officials.
  • ExpansionTreaty with Seleucus I secured the northwestern frontier and opened diplomatic exchange.
The treatise on statecraft

Chanakya & the Arthashastra

A working manual of empire — covering everything from taxation to spycraft, the Arthashastra reveals how methodically the Mauryan state was run.

Statecraft

Detailed guidance on kingship, succession, and the balance of power between ruler and council.

Administration

A structured bureaucracy of ministers, inspectors, and provincial officials reporting to the crown.

Economy

Regulation of trade, agriculture, and taxation to keep the treasury — and the army — well funded.

Diplomacy

A doctrine of alliances and rivalries among neighbouring states, weighed with cold pragmatism.

Espionage

A network of informants used to monitor officials, foreign courts, and the loyalty of the people.

Military organisation

Standing armies, fortification strategy, and rules of engagement drawn up in exacting detail.

Life Inside the Mauryan Empire

Farmers

Farmers

The backbone of the economy, farming state-owned and private land, taxed in grain to fund the empire's administration and armies.

Soldiers

Soldiers

Part of one of the largest standing armies of the ancient world, including infantry, cavalry, chariots, and war elephants.

Traders

Traders

Moved goods along royal roads and river routes, under regulated weights, measures, and tolls set by the state.

Artisans

Artisans

Organised into guilds producing textiles, metalwork, and pottery — supplying both the court and everyday markets.

Royal Court

Royal Court

Ministers, generals, and spies surrounded the emperor, advising on law, war, and the daily business of governance.

The imperial capital

Pataliputra

Set where the Son meets the Ganges, Pataliputra grew into one of the largest and most fortified cities of the ancient world.

City reconstruction

Timber palisades, moats, and towers described by foreign visitors as unmatched in scale.

Palace

A sprawling wooden royal palace, later described as rivalling the grandeur of Persian courts.

Fortifications

A ring of watchtowers and a wide moat protected the city from siege and flood alike.

Ganges / Son region

Its river confluence gave the city trade access across the empire and fertile land to sustain it.

Administrative importance

Home to the royal court, treasury, and the officials who governed the empire's provinces.

c. 268 – 232 BCE

Ashoka the Great

  • Early reign

    Ascends the throne after a disputed succession, inheriting an empire already vast in scale.

  • Expansion

    Continues Mauryan conquest, pushing the empire's borders to their greatest extent.

  • Kalinga War

    Launches a brutal campaign against the independent kingdom of Kalinga on the eastern coast.

  • Transformation

    Confronted by the war's devastation, turns away from conquest and toward a new philosophy of rule.

  • Buddhist patronage

    Sponsors the spread of Buddhism, funding monasteries, missions, and monuments across the empire.

c. 261 BCE

The Kalinga War

A single campaign that changed the character of an empire — and the mind of the man who ruled it.

01

Expansion

Ashoka's empire presses outward, with Kalinga the last major power resisting Mauryan control.

02

Kalinga

An independent, prosperous kingdom on the eastern coast, unwilling to submit without a fight.

03

War

A massive invasion force overwhelms Kalinga's defenders in one of the bloodiest campaigns of the era.

04

Human Cost

Ancient accounts describe enormous casualties and mass deportation — a scale of suffering Ashoka would later record himself.

05

Transformation

Grief and remorse turn Ashoka from further conquest toward a policy of dhamma — moral governance over force.

"What the Kalinga war could not do, Ashoka's remorse over it did — it ended the empire's age of conquest."

Edicts carved in stone

Ashoka's Dhamma

Across pillars and rock faces spanning the empire, Ashoka inscribed a code of conduct for rulers and subjects alike.

Ethical conduct

Honesty, self-restraint, and generosity urged upon officials and ordinary people alike.

Religious tolerance

Respect for all faiths practised across the empire, with instructions against sectarian conflict.

Welfare

Wells, rest houses, and hospitals for people and animals, funded along major roads.

Non-violence

A marked reduction in hunting and animal sacrifice, reflecting Ashoka's turn toward ahimsa.

Respect for traditions

Edicts calling for courtesy between different communities and schools of thought.

185 BCE and after

An Empire Disappeared. Its Symbols Survived.

The Mauryan state ended in 185 BCE — but its emblems and ideas became the foundation of a nation's identity.

Lion Capital of Ashoka

Later adopted as India's national emblem, symbolising strength and authority.

Ashoka Chakra

The 24-spoked wheel now sits at the centre of the Indian national flag.

Pillars

Scattered across the subcontinent, still among the finest surviving works of ancient Indian craftsmanship.

Buddhist heritage

Ashoka's missions helped carry Buddhism across Asia, from Sri Lanka to Central Asia.

Political & administrative legacy

Later Indian empires borrowed from Mauryan ideas of centralised bureaucracy and provincial governance.
