Before the Raj

How a trading company arrived

The East India Company

Chartered in 1600, the Company was formed by London merchants to trade in spices, textiles and other goods with Asia.

Trading posts

Fortified posts at Surat, Madras, Bombay and Calcutta grew from warehouses into small fortified towns over the 1600s.

Growing political influence

As Mughal authority weakened in the 1700s, the Company began negotiating treaties, raising local armies and taking sides in regional politics.

From Traders to Rulers

How British influence expanded

1600

Royal Charter

Queen Elizabeth I grants the East India Company a monopoly on English trade with Asia.

1757

Battle of Plassey

A decisive Company victory in Bengal marks the start of direct territorial control in India.

1765

Diwani of Bengal

The Mughal emperor grants the Company the right to collect revenue across Bengal, Bihar and Odisha.

1803

Capture of Delhi

Company forces take Delhi, bringing the ageing Mughal court under their nominal protection.

1850s

Near-total control

Through conquest, treaties and annexation, Company territory covers most of the subcontinent.

The East India Company

A company that ruled a subcontinent

Trade

Textiles, spices, indigo, tea and opium were exported through Company-controlled ports.

Military power

A private army, larger than most European states', included British officers and Indian sepoys.

Political expansion

Subsidiary alliances and annexations, such as the Doctrine of Lapse, steadily absorbed princely states.

Revenue systems

Land revenue settlements like the Permanent Settlement reshaped rural land ownership and taxation.

The Revolt of 1857

India's first major uprising against Company rule

Causes

Grievances over land revenue, the annexation of states, and the greased cartridges controversy among sepoys.

Major centres

Meerut, Delhi, Kanpur, Lucknow and Jhansi became the main centres of fighting and siege.

Key figures

Rani Lakshmibai, Bahadur Shah Zafar, Nana Sahib and Tantia Tope led resistance in different regions.

Consequences

The Company was dissolved in 1858, and the British Crown took direct control of India.

The British Raj

Crown rule, 1858 onwards

Crown rule

India was governed directly by the British Crown through a Viceroy, replacing Company administration.

Administration

A layered civil service administered provinces, districts and princely states under British oversight.

Railways

A vast rail network connected ports to inland regions, primarily to move troops, goods and raw materials.

Bureaucracy

The Indian Civil Service became the backbone of governance, though senior posts stayed largely British.

Economic changes

India shifted toward exporting raw materials and importing British manufactured goods, reshaping local industry.

India Under Colonial Rule

Click a topic to explore

India's economy was reoriented to supply raw materials to Britain and serve as a market for British manufactured goods, causing a steady drain of wealth.

Cash crops like indigo, cotton and opium were pushed over food crops, contributing to vulnerability during famines.

Traditional handicrafts and textile weaving declined under competition from machine-made British imports.

English-medium schools and universities produced a new professional class, while literacy overall stayed low.

Social reform movements, new print media and shifting caste and gender debates emerged alongside colonial rule.

Cities Transformed

Colonial port cities and capitals

Bombay

Grew from seven islands into a major textile and trading port on the western coast.

Calcutta

Served as capital of British India until 1911, and became a centre of trade and print culture.

Delhi

Became the new imperial capital in 1911, with New Delhi built as a planned administrative city.

Madras

One of the earliest Company settlements, developing into a major southern administrative centre.

The Freedom Movement

Select a movement to explore

Swadeshi · 1905

Sparked by the partition of Bengal, the Swadeshi movement promoted boycotting British goods and reviving Indian industry.

Non-Cooperation · 1920

Led by Gandhi, Indians withdrew from British institutions, schools and honours in a mass, non-violent campaign.

Civil Disobedience · 1930

The Salt March to Dandi opened a campaign of deliberately breaking unjust colonial laws.

Quit India · 1942

A mass call for immediate British withdrawal, met with widespread arrests and the final push toward independence.
