The Road to Partition

How the decision came to be

1940s

Political negotiations

Talks between the British government, the Congress and the Muslim League repeatedly failed to agree on a single constitution.

1946

Communal tensions

Riots in Calcutta, Noakhali, Bihar and Punjab hardened positions and made a unified India seem less possible by the day.

Early 1947

Independence discussions

Viceroy Mountbatten moved to transfer power quickly. Discussion shifted from "if" partition would happen to "how".

Jul–Aug 1947

The 1947 decision

The Indian Independence Act passed in July. Two dominions would exist within weeks — the border still undrawn.

The Radcliffe Line

A border drawn in five weeks

01. BOUNDARY COMMISSION — Sir Cyril Radcliffe, a lawyer who had never visited India, chairs the panel dividing Punjab and Bengal.

02. DRAWING THE BORDERS — Working from outdated maps and census data, the commission has roughly five weeks to divide mixed-population provinces.

03. ANNOUNCING THE BOUNDARIES — The line is published 17 August 1947, two days after independence, leaving millions uncertain which country they are in.

The Great Migration

Home to a new beginning

01 · Home

Families packed what they could carry, often believing the move would be temporary.

02 · Journey

On foot, by bullock cart, by train — long convoys known as kafilas stretched for miles.

03 · Border

Crossing points like Wagah became crowded, chaotic thresholds between two new nations.

04 · Refugee Camps

Makeshift camps at Kurukshetra, Walton and elsewhere sheltered hundreds of thousands.

05 · New Beginning

Over years, refugees rebuilt homes, businesses and communities in unfamiliar cities.

Trains of 1947

The railway line between two nations

Carriages built for a few hundred passengers often carried several times that number, with people riding on roofs and footboards. The railway also became a site of some of partition's worst violence — these trains remain one of the era's most haunting symbols.
